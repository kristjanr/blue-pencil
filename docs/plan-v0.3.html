Blue Pencil
Engineering plan · v0.3 12 September 2026 Status: series-agnostic draft

Blue Pencil

An engine that takes an unfinished long series and produces a continuation the author's own readers would argue about — built as an editorial pipeline, not a prompt.

Thesis Why this isn't "ask Claude to write Book 6"

The context window is working memory. The story is the database.

Every prior attempt failed the same way: plot continuity held up surprisingly well, sentence-level prose gave the game away. So the engine is designed around the failure, not the fantasy.

The arithmetic settles the architecture before any taste does. A five-book epic can run to 2.4 million tokens; a lighter five-book series like Bobiverse is around 700 thousand. The big one doesn't fit in a 1M context at all, and even the small one shouldn't be dumped in: "having read everything" is not the same as "knowing what this character currently believes, and when they could have learned it." Human readers don't carry the text either; they carry a compressed model of the world and pull specific scenes on demand. Blue Pencil does the same thing systematically.

The engine knows nothing about any particular series. Everything series-specific — how time is counted, how far apart places are, how information travels, how narration is structured, who the POV characters are — lives in a series profile the engine reads. The examples below use Bobiverse, because that's the corpus in hand; swap the profile and the same machinery runs on anything with enough books behind it.

Four commitments follow, and every later section derives from them:

  1. Extract, don't remember. The books are turned into a cited, queryable story bible once. Every generation step retrieves a small, exact context pack from it.
  2. Separate what happens from how it's written from whether it's consistent. Three different jobs, three different agents, three different failure modes — never one prompt.
  3. Backtest before you generate. Hide the last published book, continue from the one before, score against reality. The engine earns the right to write Book 6 by proving it could have written Book 5.
  4. The human is editor-in-chief. The engine produces candidates and marginalia. Taste, boredom, and "no" stay human. Invention is a tournament the human judges, not a property of the prose model.
Editor's note

The name is the design brief. A blue pencil is what an editor marks a manuscript with. Most of this engine's value is in the marking, not the typing — the continuity, voice, and repetition checkers that no previous AI-fanfic project bothered to build.

Architecture Six stages, one loop, two human gates

The pipeline

1 · Ingest Corpus → scenes EPUB to chapters to scenes. POV, date, cast, place tagged. Stable IDs. Full-text + vector index.
2 · Extract Story graph Events, observations, reports, beliefs, consequences; entities, objects, promises, threads; per-POV technique spec. Every claim cited and scored.
3 · Plan · gate Series → book → chapter cards Ending hypotheses ranked by evidence. Move tournament for surprises. You pick — or the run policy does.
4 · Draft Scene by scene Context pack (~40k tokens) assembled per scene. 2–4 candidates. Cached prefix.
5 · Check Blue pencils Continuity, epistemics, voice drift, repetition, style metrics, discriminator, reader panel. Marginalia with severity.
6 · Accept · gate Commit state Accepted chapter is re-extracted; deltas written to the graph. The world advances. Git-versioned.
Loop: 5 → 4 until clean or escalated · 6 → 3 for the next chapter card Branches: alternate continuations are git branches of the graph Backtest harness wraps 2–6 with a hidden book
Configuration What's series-specific, what's the user's call

Two config files, no hard-coded series

Everything a previous version of this plan phrased as "a decision to make before building" is, on reflection, a setting. There are two kinds, and they're deliberately kept apart: what is true about the series, and what the user wants from this run.

The series profile

Declares the shape of the world so the checkers can reason about it. Most of it is inferred from the corpus in Stage 1 and confirmed by a human; the rest is a few lines of YAML.

# profiles/bobiverse.yaml
narration:
  mode: first_person_rotating        # or third_close_rotating, frame, single
  pov_from: chapter_header           # how POV is signalled in the text
time:
  calendar: gregorian_offset         # years, real dates; ASOIAF would be "AC"
space:
  model: interstellar                # or travel_table, single_city, none
  distances: data/systems.csv        # light-years between named systems
information:
  channels:
    - {name: radio,  speed: c}           # light-lag applies
    - {name: SCUT,   speed: instant, available_from: "book 2"}
entities:
  aliases: data/aliases.yaml         # Will/Riker, Bob-1/Bob/original
  clone_lineage: true                # beliefs fork at replication
style:
  units: chapter                     # what the technique spec is measured over

The information.channels block is the whole reason the profile exists: it turns "could this character know this yet?" into a computation rather than a judgment. For Bobiverse that's light-lag versus SCUT. For a court intrigue it's ravens, riders and roads. For a small-town novel it's gossip and it's instant.

The run policy

Everything about how a run behaves. Each setting has a default; none is baked in.

# runs/book6-draft.yaml
gates:
  plan:    manual                # manual | auto_if_clean | auto | every_n: 5
  chapter: auto_if_clean         # auto-accept when every hard check passes
  act:     manual                # always stop at act breaks
candidates:
  per_scene: 3
  revision_rounds: 2
context:
  start_tokens: 30000
  max_tokens:   50000
models:
  plan: claude-fable-5-1
  draft: claude-opus-5
  extract: claude-sonnet-5
  checks: claude-haiku-4-5
checks:
  epistemic:  hard
  geography:  hard
  repetition: {severity: soft, budget: corpus_baseline}
  discriminator: floor
budget:
  max_usd: 400

So "how much do you want to judge" is answered per run, and can change mid-book: full manual for the first act while you're calibrating trust, auto_if_clean once the checkers have earned it, manual again for the ending. The editor doesn't have to decide their working style before the tool exists.

Stage 1 Ingestion

Corpus → scenes

Input is a directory of EPUBs. Output is a SQLite database with one row per scene, plus an FTS5 full-text index and an embedding index (sqlite-vec) over chunks of 300–800 tokens.

Scene boundaries come from the text itself (section breaks, POV headers) with a model pass to split long chapters where the break is implicit. Each scene carries metadata the later stages query on:

  • book · chapter · scene · pov — the address every citation uses
  • date_inworld — a best-estimate in-world date in the profile's calendar, with an uncertainty range, so the time and information-path checks have something to work with
  • cast · place — who is on the page and where
  • tokens · words — so pacing baselines can be computed per POV

If a volume exists only as audio, ingestion gains a transcription step, and two things get harder: chapter headers and paragraph breaks are gone, so scene segmentation leans on the model pass; and proper nouns come out mangled, so a normalisation pass matches transcribed names against the entity list built from the earlier volumes before anything else runs.

Nothing clever happens here on purpose. Ingestion is deterministic and re-runnable; every downstream artefact is derived from it and can be regenerated.

Stage 2 The story graph — cited, scored, never silently resolved

The story graph

This is the expensive one-time step and the thing that makes everything else cheap. Extraction runs as batch jobs over every scene with structured outputs, so each record is schema-valid and carries the scene ID and quote that supports it. A claim with no citation is rejected at write time — but a citation only proves the text contains evidence related to the claim, not that the claim is right. So every record also carries a claim type (explicit · inferred · disputed), a confidence, and any alternative readings. Contradictions between scenes are stored as unresolved records with both interpretations, never overwritten: in a series built on unreliable narrators, the contradiction is often the story.

The fundamental object is not a fact but an event, with the paths by which knowledge of it travelled. That is what lets a checker answer the question that matters — "is there an information path from this event to this character?" — rather than merely "is this true?"

Entities

Characters, factions, places, objects. Aliases resolved from the profile ("Will", "Riker", "Bob-3" are one node) so retrieval doesn't miss a scene because of a nickname. Physical and embodied state tracked — for a series where minds move between bodies, "where is this character" and "what hardware are they running on" are separate fields.

Timeline

Ordered events with in-world dates and dependencies. Also what each POV character believes happened — two Bobs in different systems hold different timelines of the same war, and both differ from the reader's.

Event & belief graph

The single most important structure. Each event has observers (who was there), reports (who told whom, when, over which channel, and how distorted), and per-character beliefs — including false ones — plus what the reader has been shown. Beliefs are dated and versioned, so "what did Riker believe on the day of chapter 47" is a query. When a character is cloned, the clone inherits the parent's beliefs as of that moment and diverges from there. Continuity checking in Stage 5 is mostly path queries against this graph, using the profile's information channels.

Consequences

Narrative physics. Every major event carries cause and consequence edges: what it cost, which options it closed, which obligations it created, which promises it made impossible. The planner runs a consequence audit — an accepted event that changes nothing downstream is flagged as inert. This is the difference between a novel whose 400th page happens because of its 100th and five hundred pages of competent scenes.

Relationships

Directed edges with sentiment, obligation, and history. "Owes a debt", "swore an oath", "suspects", "loved once". Edges are dated so they can change.

Object registry

Chekhov's inventory. Named swords, letters, eggs, horns, coins — where each one is, who holds it, and whether the text has flagged it as significant. A sword can't be in two places.

Promise ledger

Every prophecy, vow, foreshadowing beat, and dangling setup, with a confidence score and the scenes that plant it. This is the raw material for invention: a good twist cashes a promise the text already made.

Open threads

Plotlines with their last known state and the characters attached. The book outline in Stage 3 is essentially a schedule for advancing these.

Technique spec, per POV

Two layers. The measured layer: sentence-length distribution, dialogue ratio, sensory density, interiority ratio — numbers the voice checker can compute. The mechanism layer: narrative distance, how exposition is delivered (Bill: as an engineer's project log with asides; Riker: through the grind of Earth politics), how emotion is disclosed (deflected into a pop-culture reference, usually), recurring devices (the running gag, the self-aware aside, the moral inventory). Canon exemplars are tagged with these mechanisms and with situation, so a Bill scene mid-build retrieves Bill-mid-build and Bill-deflecting-with-a-joke. When POVs are clones of one voice, the spec records what drifted — that's the series' own story. The target is not "sounds like the author"; it is "behaves like this POV in this situation."

What a record looks like

// one event in the graph, with its information paths
{
  "event_id": "E-0311",
  "summary": "Milo is destroyed by a Brazilian probe at Omicron² Eridani",
  "when": {"date": "<profile calendar>", "certainty": "canonical"},
  "where": "Omicron² Eridani",
  "observed_by": ["Milo"],                       // final transmission only
  "reports": [
    {"from": "Milo", "to": "Bill", "channel": "radio",
     "arrives": "<computed: distance / c>", "status": "explicit"},
    {"from": "Bill", "to": "Riker", "channel": "radio",
     "arrives": "<computed>", "status": "inferred", "confidence": 0.7}
  ],
  "beliefs": {
    "Bill":  {"state": "knows",   "as_of": "<arrival date>"},
    "Riker": {"state": "unaware", "as_of": "<before second arrival>"}
  },
  "reader_state": "shown directly",
  "consequences": ["Omicron² Eridani colonisation blocked",
                   "Brazilian probe threat confirmed for every Bob",
                   "promise P-0021 (Milo's survey) closed"],
  "claim_type": "explicit",
  "citations": [{"scene": "WAL.xx.Milo", "quote": "..."}]
}

The arrives fields are not extracted; they're computed from the profile's space model and channel speeds. That is the whole trick: the checker doesn't ask a model whether Riker could know yet. It does the arithmetic.

Extraction is deliberately over-inclusive on the first pass and pruned by a second, cheaper pass that merges duplicates, scores confidence, and records contradictions between scenes as open records rather than resolving them.

Stage 3 Planning — where surprise actually comes from

Planning is a tournament, not a prompt

Prior attempts asked the model for an outline and took the first answer. That produces the median continuation — competent, predictable, slightly dull. Blue Pencil treats planning as search with a human judge.

Series thesis

Before any chapter is planned, the engine drafts several ending hypotheses — full-series resolutions — and scores each on evidence from the promise ledger: how many planted setups it pays off, how many it orphans, whether it fits the author's public statements and the structural symmetries the text has already set up. The human picks one (or edits one). Everything downstream serves it.

Book skeleton

The book outline mirrors the series' actual shape, computed from the corpus rather than assumed: chapter count, POV slate and rotation rhythm, act breaks, the ratio of quiet chapters to turning points. Every long series has a measurable cadence — how often it cuts between POVs, how many plotlines it keeps in the air — and the skeleton copies it.

Move tournament

For each open thread at each act break, a generator proposes eight to twelve candidate moves — what could happen next. Each is scored by separate judges on:

  • Setup — does it cash promises already in the ledger?
  • Cost — is it irreversible? Does someone lose something real?
  • Character truth — would this person do this, given their ledger entries?
  • Inevitable in hindsight — surprising now, obvious later. The Red Wedding test.
  • Futures opened — how many interesting states does this move create? "A and B agree to cooperate" opens almost nothing. "A learns B's secret, B realises A knows, both pretend otherwise" opens a dozen. This is the closest thing to a computable definition of interesting.

At act breaks the search goes two moves deep: each surviving move is expanded into its own next moves, and a move is scored partly on the quality of the futures it leads to. It is not Monte Carlo in any rigorous sense, but it is search rather than sampling, and it stops the engine choosing a good scene that leads nowhere.

The top three go to the human with their scores, their consequence edges, and the citations behind them. This is the one place invention happens, and it's the place a human should spend their attention.

Chapter cards

A chapter card is the contract between planning and drafting. It is a structured record, not prose: POV, in-world date, location, cast present, the character's goal, the turn, what is revealed and to whom (this writes to the belief graph on acceptance), threads advanced, seeds planted, seeds paid, consequences expected, and the word budget. The drafter may not add a reveal the card doesn't contain.

Stage 4 Drafting — assembling the context pack

Drafting

Each scene is written from a context pack assembled by queries rather than by dumping books in. It starts at the policy's start_tokens (default 30k) — hard state, immediate narrative context, a handful of canon exemplars, technique guidance, constraints — and grows toward max_tokens only when a checker's marginalia asks for more (an information-path failure pulls in the relevant channel and distance data; a voice-drift failure pulls in more exemplars). More context is not free: past a point it is a conference room full of documents for one conversation, and the backtest ablations decide where that point is. The pack has a fixed order so its stable front half is cached across every call in the book:

// context pack, in render order — stable prefix first
[cached]
  engine rules + house style (what the engine must never do)
  series bible digest            // ~8k tokens, regenerated per act
  style spec for this POV        // numbers + exemplar constructions
[per chapter]
  chapter card
  POV character state: location, body/hardware, knows / believes-false, goals
  4–8 canon exemplars: same POV, similar situation + technique tags   // voice by retrieval, not fine-tuning
  summaries of the previous 3 chapters + the previous chapter in this POV
  phrase ledger: distinctive phrases used in the last 40k words  // do not reuse
[per scene]
  scene beats · word budget · "write scene 2 of 4"

Voice comes from retrieved exemplars — canon passages in the same POV, matched on situation and on the technique tags from the spec, not on surface similarity. The exemplars show how this POV handles this kind of moment; the technique spec says why. This is few-shot by retrieval, it beats "write like the author" instructions by a wide margin, and nothing is trained on the text.

Every scene is drafted candidates.per_scene times. Candidates are ranked by the Stage 5 judges before a human ever sees them, so the human reads the best one and the marginalia, not a pile.

Model defaults

The run policy sets a model per stage; the defaults are Claude Opus 5 with adaptive thinking at high effort for drafting and chapter planning, and Claude Fable 5.1 for the move tournament and series thesis — the hardest judgment calls. Long drafts stream with a 128k output ceiling. Chapter cards and every graph record use structured outputs so nothing downstream parses prose. Fiction gets violent; the pipeline handles the refusal stop reason with server-side fallbacks rather than crashing mid-chapter.

Stage 5 The blue pencils

Editorial checks

Each checker reads a draft plus the graph and emits marginalia: a location in the text, a severity, a reason, and where possible the citation it conflicts with. Hard failures send the scene back to Stage 4 with the marginalia attached. Soft ones accumulate for the human as a scorecard — continuity, character fidelity, causal coherence, surprise, payoff, prose, repetition, exposition — that is never collapsed into one number. A single score hides exactly the trade-offs an editor needs to see.

Epistemic check hardDoes any character act on, mention, or react to an event no information path could have delivered to them by this date? The classic AI-fanfic failure — a Bob reacting to news that is still years out in transit — is a query against the graph and the profile's channels, not a judgment call.
Geography & time hardCan this character physically be here on this date, given where the last accepted chapter left them and the profile's space model — travel tables, or distance over speed?
Object & body check hardDead characters stay dead, unless the profile says backups exist — and then the restore has to be on the page. Named objects are where the registry says they are.
Card compliance hardThe scene contains the reveals on the card and no others. Uncarded reveals are the easiest way to corrupt the ledger.
Repetition auditor soft → hardN-gram and motif frequencies compared against the canon baseline for this POV and against the phrase ledger of recent chapters. Every series has its "cold, wind, darkness, blood" — the auditor computes the budget from the corpus rather than guessing it, and exceeding it is a failure, not a note.
Voice drift softSentence-length distribution, interiority ratio, dialogue ratio, sensory density measured against this POV's style spec. Reports the distance, not an opinion.
Discriminator floor onlyA judge is shown canon and generated passages unlabeled and asked which is which. High confidence flags a scene; low confidence proves nothing — bland prose is also hard to tell from mediocre canon. This is a floor against obviously machine-made text, never an optimisation target. Optimising for it produces beige.
Consequence audit softDoes the scene generate the consequences its card promised? Does anything in it change a future state? A scene that leaves the world exactly as it found it is questioned, not cut — quiet chapters exist — but it has to be quiet on purpose.
Reader panel softThree personas — a first-time reader, a wiki-editing superfan, a line editor — each score the scene for boredom, exposition, and payoff. Their disagreements are the interesting output.
Continuity violation — example marginalium

ch17.s2 · line 41 — Riker refers to Milo's destruction. Event E-0311: the only channel from Omicron² Eridani at this date is radio; earliest arrival at Sol is <computed>, after this chapter's date. Riker's belief state is unaware. Fixes: (1) move the line to a chapter after arrival, (2) route the news through a Bob who could have it and put that on the page, (3) cut it.

Stage 6 Accepting a chapter moves the world

State commit

When a chapter is accepted — by a person, or by the run policy when every hard check passes — the extraction agents from Stage 2 run on the generated text exactly as they ran on canon, and the resulting deltas — new events, new reports and beliefs, new wounds, new promises, consequences, threads closed — are written to the graph with citations pointing at the accepted chapter. From then on retrieval treats it as canon.

This is what makes chapter 60 consistent with chapter 3 without either of them being in context. The graph is the memory; the chapters are its journal. And because beliefs are dated, the state of the world immediately before any chapter is reconstructible — "what did every Bob believe going into chapter 47" is a checkout, not a guess.

The graph lives as markdown and SQLite in a git repository. Each accepted chapter is a commit. Alternate continuations are branches, and a wrong turn in chapter 30 is a reset, not a rewrite. Human edits to any graph file are first-class — if you correct a belief by hand, the engine works from your correction.

Evaluation Backtest before you write the unknown

The backtest, with a contamination control

Everything above could be built and still produce plausible garbage. The way to know is to hide a book that exists — but there is a catch v0.1 missed: the model may have read it. Any series famous enough to have a wiki is in every frontier model's training data. Score "did we reproduce the real book" naively and the benchmark looks spectacular for the wrong reason.

So the backtest has a control and three tiers. The development corpus is Bobiverse: books one through five are the training side, the newest volume is the hidden book.

Control: what does the model already know?

Before any run, the model is asked — with no corpus, no graph — to outline the hidden book chapter by chapter. Whatever it recalls is the contamination baseline. Plot recall from the full pipeline only counts to the extent it beats that baseline, and any event the bare model already knew is excluded from the score. This doesn't make the corpus clean; it makes the contamination measurable.

Tier A — shape

Does the generated book have the series' proportions? POV distribution, chapter-length distribution, plotlines running in parallel, geographic spread, threads opened versus closed, setup-to-payoff ratio, cadence of turning points. These are compared to the real hidden book and can't be gamed by memory — the model can't recite a distribution.

Tier B — constraints

Given only the earlier books, does the generated one obey every constraint a legitimate next volume must? Every belief has an information path. Nobody is in two places. Dead stays dead. Objects stay where they were put. This is the graph and the checkers scoring themselves, and it needs no answer key at all.

Tier C — genuinely blind

A hidden book the model cannot have seen: one published after its training cutoff. Continue from the volume before it; score against the real one. This is the only tier that tests prediction rather than reconstruction, and one honest run of it is worth more than any number of runs on a famous series.

The newest Bobiverse volume is the candidate. The contamination probe decides whether it qualifies — a bare-model outline that comes back empty is the admission ticket. If it passes, the same corpus serves all three tiers, and it is an unusually good one:

  • Light-lag is a native information-path test. Bobs in different systems learn of events years apart before SCUT relays and instantly after; the belief graph's "is there a path from this event to this character by this date" query has the story's own physics behind it.
  • Many first-person POVs that began as one person. Replicative drift is the series' own version of voice drift. The technique spec has to separate Bill from Riker from Howard — a harder, more honest style test than two characters who were never alike.
  • Five prior books, roughly half a million words, about 700k tokens. The entire series fits in a single 1M context, which unlocks an ablation a bigger series can't offer: whole series in context versus the graph. If the graph doesn't beat brute force where brute force is possible, that's worth knowing in week nine, not week thirty.
  • Comedic, reference-dense, present-day idiom stresses the technique spec somewhere third-person grimdark never would.

Across all tiers: repetition index against the real book's own baseline, and blind human ratings — a small panel reads unlabeled chapters, real and generated. That last one is still the number that ultimately matters.

The backtest is also the development loop. Ablations — drop the belief graph, drop retrieved exemplars, drop the repetition auditor, halve the context pack — show which parts of the engine are earning their cost. Spend fifty dollars finding out whether a checker helps before spending a week making it cheaper.

Editor's note

If the probe shows the model already knows the new volume, Tiers A and B still run on it — shape and constraints don't need blindness beyond the baseline — and Tier C goes shopping for another post-cutoff series. Tier C is where the engine is actually on trial; nothing else in the plan substitutes for it.

Roadmap Each phase has an exit test

Build order

The order changed in v0.2: the Continuity Editor ships before any generation exists. It is useful on its own — point it at a human-written fan chapter and it marks it up — and it is the cheapest possible proof that the story graph works. If the graph can't catch a planted error in a chapter someone else wrote, there is no point drafting one.

Risks Named now so they're not discovered later

What could still go wrong

Invention ceiling

The tournament raises the floor; it may not raise the ceiling. If the backtest shows the real book's biggest turns never appear in the top three, the human's role shifts from judge to co-author, and the engine becomes an editor for a human plot. That is still a good product.

Graph errors compound

A wrong belief entry propagates into every chapter after it, and a citation does not make a claim true. Mitigations: confidence and claim-type on every record, contradictions kept open, the Phase 1 audit, the planted-error test in Phase 2, and the reader-panel superfan persona whose entire job is to catch this.

Benchmark contamination

Any famous series is in the training data; a good backtest score on one can be memory wearing a lab coat. Mitigations: the contamination probe, shape and constraint tiers that memory can't game, and one genuinely post-cutoff corpus.

Living authors, unfinished work

This is fan work for private reading, and several of the authors whose series make good corpora are on record against AI use of their books. The engine builds a technique specification from published text and retrieves exemplars at runtime; it does not fine-tune on the books, and nothing it produces is for distribution. Keep it that way.

Profile errors

If the profile says a channel is instant when the books treat it as delayed, every information-path check is confidently wrong. Mitigation: the profile is inferred from the corpus and shown to a human with the evidence, and the planted-error test in Phase 2 includes profile-level mistakes.

Refusals mid-chapter

The genre is violent. Plan for the refusal stop reason with fallbacks and scene-level retries; never let a refused scene silently become a blank in the manuscript.

Human attention

Two gates per chapter across 70+ chapters is a real commitment. The review page has to make a chapter reviewable in ten minutes, or the gates get rubber-stamped and the whole design fails quietly.

Decisions needed Before Phase 0 starts

Two things only you can decide

  1. The target series. The development and backtest corpus is Bobiverse, because it's in hand and it's a good test. The series you actually want to continue is a separate question and only needs answering by Phase 6 — it gets its own profile and nothing else changes. One neat option: if the newest Bobiverse volume proves blind, the same corpus can graduate from test subject to target — prove the engine on book six, then point it at book seven.
  2. The blind corpus — provisionally settled. The newest Bobiverse volume is the candidate, pending the contamination probe. If the bare model can outline it, it's burnt and the search for a post-cutoff series resumes; if it can't, Tier C is funded and the only honest test in the plan has its subject.

Two earlier "decisions" are gone. How much the human judges is run policy, changeable per run and per act. Whether the editor ships alone is the plan: Stages 2, 5 and 6 work on any manuscript, including a human's fan continuation, and they ship in Phase 2 as a tool in their own right. The generator follows only once the editor has proved the graph.
