There was a definite smell of grass and pollen in the air, an odor I'd always associated with spring. What made it really special at the moment was that I was on board the Fargo, Will's traveling O'Neill cylinder. Once upon a time, we'd have thought of it as an other's cargo ship wannabe, but he'd terraformed and spun up the inside

and now it contained the 30,000 or so people we'd liberated from Romulus. Most were our descendants but there was a reasonable minority that had just joined Ever Onward because it sounded cool. I looked up at the hologramatic sky which was as blue as any summer in Minnesota back in the day.

Will had stolen the idea from Heaven's River mostly because the design of the Fargo put a large central cylinder overhead that frankly would make a person feel a little claustrophobic. I stepped to the side of the path and sat down on a convenient bench then just people watched for a while. 30,000 people had left Romulus with only what they could carry.

These days, of course, 3D printers ensured that no one went without the essentials, but the feeling of being uprooted hadn't changed. These people had no home, unless we either found them a planet to settle on or made the Fargo their permanent residence. Will wasn't really averse to the latter option.

Wormholes had completely changed the colonization business plan with the travel time down to days once the wormhole had been set up. We would be meeting with the senior members of Ever Onward to discuss this very thing. It would also be set up as a virtual town hall where people would be able to follow with their phones or Huey headsets, depending on preference.

Speaking of which, I checked my heads up. Time to go.

The conference room contained, as physical presences, myself, Will, and eight members of Ever Onward who had volunteered or were chosen to be here. The room also contained the new augmented reality projectors so that everyone with headsets could feel they were in the room with us. Projection was turned off, of course.

We didn't need 30,000 overlapping holograms vying for a chance to speak. Kalman Gupta was spokesman for the group. He started by saying a few words, welcoming everyone.

Basically a bunch of political filler. I tuned out almost immediately, trusting my replicants' ability to frame-jack and rewind if I miss the transition to the real substance. Finally, he hit that point.

Will, we have seemingly a surfeit of choices for colonization targets. You've obviously been busy over the years, both you individually and the Bobs in general. We've studied the provided options in focus groups and general discussions, and the biggest complaint, or maybe concern would be a better word,

is that no matter how we cut it, we'd be starting over. Well, yes, of course, Will replied, looking slightly mystified. I found that interesting.

Will getting caught by surprise was a rarity. Colonization is always like that, he continued. You have a certain allowance for personal items, but even those mostly can be scanned before departure and reprinted at destination.

And everything else is available as well, so I guess I'm missing the real objection here. Kalman sighed and looked down, apparently gathering his thoughts or picking his phrasing. It's not about the things we lose or leave behind.

It's not even about the work. Look, when you move to a new city, even if you bring all your possessions with you, you still feel dislocated. You can get over it eventually, but you have to live through that period of feeling lost, of not belonging.

Excuse me, I said, cutting in, but isn't everyone on the Fargo a member of Ever Onward? I mean, to join in the first place, you had to be thinking in those terms, at least in principle. For some, Kalman replied, but I think we can agree that the society was actually set up to gather and organize the Johansson descendants.

I know for a fact that anyone related to you was specifically contacted about membership. Still, about 8% of the membership are not your relatives, Bob. Not really my point, Kalman.

The charter of Ever Onward was to discuss and consider emigration from Romulus for those who didn't feel they fit in with the government's policies. We don't need to go into that in detail, and I certainly don't want to get into a religious or moral debate. I paused and cocked my head.

But if you joined, the understanding was that you had concerns about staying. Will cut in at that point. And yes, we developed it primarily as a convenient way to organize and identify our descendants, but keep in mind, not all our descendants came along, and we've never turned anyone away.

One of the other representatives put up a hand and spoke before anyone could formally recognize her. I feel like we're all talking past each other. There's a lot of not my point and it's not about going back and forth.

Regardless of intentions, plans, or whatever, most of the membership is feeling, um, lost, for want of a better word, like refugees.

Do you have any kind of consensus on what might be an acceptable resolution? Will asked. The woman, belatedly metadata popped up, identifying her as Marsha Wallington, a Johansson descendant, pressed her lips together in a thin line before replying,

We have many consensuses, which is just a way of saying no, we don't. I think if a vote was taken today, though, the winner would be for us to be let off on one of the settled worlds to be integrated with their society.

At one end of the room, a scrolling window in AR showed comments by viewers in real time. It was impossible to keep up with while paying attention to the discussion in the room, but I could tell when things got more contentious because the scrolling speed increased.

30,000 people dumped into a stable population might be a little much, Will said. Most of the colony worlds aren't really colonial anymore. They're settled.

and well past the Wild West phase.

And they might not be happy to be asked to absorb that many people, Kalman finished for Will, nodding. The problem is that Clan Johansson is really a thing. Your descendants identify as a cohesive group, and I don't think they'd take well to being split up across multiple worlds.

Well, the 8 percent. Sure, sure, Kalman said, waving me off. But that still leaves 92 percent as a block.

It's still much the same issue. So can we try to articulate exactly what the issue is, Will said. I believe we have, Will.

We- No, we haven't, Kalman. We've kind of danced around it, but it sounds to me, and I don't mean this in a disparaging way, although it'll come across like that, that people don't want to be pioneers doing pioneering things. Kalman looked down for a few moments, his lips burst in thought.

That's very reductionist, but not wrong in its essentials. Okay, look, Marcia cut in again. Is there any way we can reduce this to a limited number of scenarios, then vote on them?

Nothing binding, I just think we need to identify what we do want, rather than going on about what we don't.

rounded each other, then one by one began to nod. Excellent suggestion, Marsha, Kalman said. Let's open it up wide.

Everyone can suggest whatever they want. We'll do a round of consolidation of very similar ideas, then a series of elimination votes to get it down to manageable numbers, say the top 20, then ranked voting to get the top three and a final runoff. The AR comments section began to zoom by faster than I'd yet seen it.

I couldn't tell if it was mostly pro or con, but the suggestion definitely had everyone interested. Well, let's set it up, Will said. After the town hall I popped into Will's VR.

He seemed to be going through a whimsical period and was currently living in a castle, although the design leaned more toward fairy tale and away from defensive redoubt. In particular, the balcony had a great view of the valley. I spent a few mills admiring that view while Will paged through a data window.

Is this your kingdom? I asked as I walked over to join him. If you can have a kingdom without people, sure, he replied.

Bob, the last thing I want is more people to worry about even in VR. I nodded in understanding and pulled up a chair. Will truly had burned out after decades of managing the evacuation of Earth.

I could never figure out why he kept getting involved in projects when he would then proceed to complain. Constantly.

I was probably asking for trouble but couldn't resist a comment.

And your solution to this is to take on the resettlement of the ever-onward people. Will snorted. Well, they are mostly our descendants and yes, I have a soft spot for them.

Still, I'm not going to do this alone.

Who's helping then? You. I couldn't help laughing.

Okay, fair enough, we both have a soft spot. I gestured at the data window. Learned anything?

Will stopped scrolling and sat back. Interesting thing. Lots of acrimony, people attacking anyone they don't agree with, typical social media anonymous behavior, except, he paused, there are people pushing for resettlement of Earth, and they aren't being attacked, at least not as much.

And this tells you what? That people have a sort of respect for that option, even if they're pushing for an alternative. I thought about that for a moment.

How would you feel about that, Will? Honestly, he looked up at me, then back at the data window. I'd be completely fine with it.

I know I said earlier that I'd be okay with them staying in the Fargo, but I'm having second thoughts. I want to be done, Bob. Get them settled somewhere, then leave and have nothing more to do with humans.

Wow, you really are burned out. Call it what you want, I'm done. You might want to think about whether

you want to come along on what might be a beeline for the far reaches. It's not the worst idea, I said. Will looked up again and this time swiveled his chair to face me.

Bob, have you ever wondered if there's a pattern we're following? How so?

The PGF had digitals, replicants. Then they had some kind of a falling out. I wonder if that sort of thing is inevitable.

Maybe there's a pattern. Maybe there's a pattern to history. Maybe we're just not compatible with bios in the long term.

I stared at him for a moment. Wow, you are morose today. Maybe.

But I've been spending a lot of time thinking about this. I wish we could get more information from the archivist. I think we might be heading for a confrontation.

The singularity? Sure, why not? The whole point of the singularity has always been that we couldn't predict what it would be.

Maybe it's just an acrimonious divorce. Well, that's depressing. Thanks, Will.

I'm gonna go get drunk now. Anytime, Bob.
